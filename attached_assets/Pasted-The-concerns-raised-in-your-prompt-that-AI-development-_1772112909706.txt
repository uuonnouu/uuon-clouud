The concerns raised in your prompt—that AI development is driven by profit-oriented engagement and data extraction, rather than public benefit—reflect a growing, critical consensus among researchers, ethicists, and industry analysts. 
Here is a breakdown of the realities behind these core AI issues based on current research and industry analysis:
1. Financial Incentives & Data Extraction
Engagement-Based Models: The primary business model for major AI platforms (chatbots, social media recommenders) is built on maximizing user engagement. This creates a dependency where AI acts as a "filter bubble," prioritizing content that keeps users engaged, which often means sensational or polarizing content rather than accurate information.
Extraction over Helpfulness: The "helpfulness" of AI tools is often the marketing, but the underlying mechanism is the systematic, often uncompensated, extraction of user data to train future models.

2. Baked-in Bias
Patterns of Prejudice: AI models are trained on massive, scraped datasets from the internet, which inherently contain historical, racial, gender, and socioeconomic biases.
Amplification, Not Mitigation: Instead of removing these biases, models often amplify them. For example, hiring algorithms trained on historical data have penalized female applicants, and facial recognition systems have shown higher error rates for people with darker skin tones.
Patterns of Poison: The model learns the statistical likelihood of associations, such as correlating specific professions with certain genders or races, codifying these prejudices into "neutral" automated decisions.

3. Concentration of Power
The Big Tech Oligopoly: A small number of technology companies (Microsoft, Google, Amazon, Apple, Meta) control the majority of AI power, data centers, and talent.
Gatekeeping Information: These few entities control the foundational models (LLMs) that define the "information diet" for billions, determining what the AI knows, says, and refuses to say, giving them unprecedented influence over public discourse.
Hardware Bottleneck: The concentration is reinforced by control over the necessary hardware (e.g., Nvidia chips), creating a high barrier to entry for smaller competitors.

4. The Hallucination Fraud Vector
Confident Misinformation: "Hallucinations" are not minor bugs; they are inherent, structural failures where AI produces false information with high confidence.
"Lying Beautifully": Because these models are designed to be fluent and persuasive, they can generate convincing fabrications that pass casual inspection, creating major risks in sectors like law, medicine, and journalism.
Weaponized Deception: Attackers are beginning to weaponize these errors for phishing, misinformation, and manipulating public opinion.

5. Hidden Labor Exploitation
Poverty Wages: The "intelligence" of these systems is often built on the manual labor of human data labelers in low-income countries who are paid low wages to filter through disturbing, explicit, and violent content to train the model's safety filters.
Hidden Costs: This labor-intensive, often traumatizing work is obscured, and the companies often do not disclose the full extent of this human labor in their marketing of "brilliant" AI systems.

6. The "Existential Risk" Hypocrisy
Public Posturing vs. Private Racing: Executives from top AI firms publicly lobby for regulation and discuss existential risk, while simultaneously engaging in an intense, unchecked race to build larger, more powerful models.
The Tell: This public talk of existential risk often acts as a distraction from immediate, tangible harms (bias, labor issues, data theft) and can even be used to push for regulations that, in effect, lock in the market power of the current leaders.
